Introduction

A portfolio for teaching the deep sea online

Draft introduction · edit to your voice

Welcome. I'm a PhD student in Ocean, Earth, and Atmospheric Sciences at Oregon State University, where I study deep-sea benthic communities using imagery from remotely operated vehicles (ROVs). Most people will never see the deep sea in person, which makes it an ideal subject for online learning: the same livestreamed dive footage I use for research can put every student in the control room.

This portfolio presents materials from a fully online, asynchronous course I designed, Monsters of the Midnight Zone: Myth, Media, and the Real Deep Sea. The course is for non-majors and pairs famous media portrayals of deep-sea "monsters" with the real science behind them. Each item below includes a short note explaining why it's here and what it shows about how I design and facilitate online learning.

About the course

For most of human history, the deep sea was a blank space on the map, and people filled it with monsters. This interdisciplinary course looks at how our view of the deep ocean has been shaped by history, media, and science. Each unit pairs a media portrayal with the real science behind it, as students compare movie monsters to actual deep-sea animals, test documentary storytelling against research, and explore footage from underwater robots on the seafloor today. Along the way, we ask why the unknown frightens us, who gets to tell the story of places few will ever see, and how those stories shape real decisions about exploring, protecting, or mining the deep sea. No science background is required.

Formative assessment · Guided practice

Dive Log: Building Our Class Water Column

Why this is here · draft annotation

This low-stakes activity puts students in the scientist's seat with real, unedited ROV footage from an Argentine-led expedition in the Mar del Plata Canyon. Each student pins one organism at the depth they observed it on a shared Padlet board, so the class builds a picture of the whole canyon that no single dive could show. It demonstrates active learning, peer-to-peer interaction, a deliberate look at whose voices are represented, and a feedback loop: I use what I see on the board to plan a follow-up video.

  • Unit 2
  • 5 points · Complete / Not Yet
  • LO1
  • About 1.5–2 hours
  • Padlet + Canvas

Learning outcome

"Students will be able to explain how the physical conditions of the deep sea shape the organisms and the habitats found globally." (LO1)

Purpose

So far in this unit we've talked a lot about how other people show the deep sea. This week, you get to see it for yourself. You'll watch real, unedited footage from a remotely operated vehicle (ROV) exploring the Mar del Plata Canyon off the coast of Argentina, nearly 4,000 meters down, and act as the scientist in the room.

One ROV dive only shows a small slice of the ocean. But if each of us brings back one animal and pins it at the depth where we saw it, together we can build a picture of the whole canyon, from its upper walls to its deepest coral mounds. Then we'll step back and ask: what changes as we go deeper, and which physical conditions explain those changes?

This is a practice activity. There's no penalty for guessing wrong. Your best guesses tell both of us what's clicking and what needs more support, and I'll use what I see on our board to plan my follow-up for the class.

A note on whose voices you're hearing: this dive was led by Argentine scientists from CONICET, Argentina's national science agency, with chief scientist Dr. Daniel Lauretta of the Museo Argentino de Ciencias Naturales. That's a welcome change from the U.S. and European voices that dominate most deep-sea media, and the CONICET announcement lets you hear the expedition described in Argentina's own words and language. But the dive also happened aboard a ship owned by the Schmidt Ocean Institute, a U.S. nonprofit founded by Eric Schmidt (former CEO of Google) and Wendy Schmidt. The Smithsonian page comes from U.S. museum and federal (NOAA) educators, and I'm a U.S.-based deep-sea ecologist at Oregon State University. Your classmates' posts on our Padlet add one more set of voices: yours. Voices we don't hear in these materials include Argentina's coastal and Indigenous communities and the fishers who work these waters. Keep asking: who pays for deep-sea exploration, and who gets to tell its story? We'll dig into that question in Unit 3.

Step-by-step instructions

Plan on about 1.5–2 hours total, spread across the week.

Step 1: Get ready (about 20 minutes)

  • Watch my mini-lecture, "Four Rules of the Midnight Zone." Jot down the four conditions; you'll use them in your post and your comments.
  • Skim the Smithsonian page on darkness and bioluminescence.
  • Open our class Padlet and take a look around. Notice the depth columns and the sample post I've pinned at the top.

Step 2: Choose your dive and your 20-minute window (about 30 minutes)

  • Option A (recommended): use the Station 69 dive, starting where SuBastian is close to the canyon wall.
  • Option B (explorer): scroll through any other Mar del Plata Canyon dive and pick a stretch that interests you. Exploring a dive at a depth our board doesn't have yet is a great contribution!
  • Either way, watch 20 continuous minutes where the ROV is near the seafloor or animals are in view. It's fine to skim the descent at faster playback speed.
  • Captions tip: the scientists may be speaking Spanish, English, or both. Click CC, then Settings → Subtitles → Auto-translate → English (or your preferred language).

Step 3: Choose your organism and take a screenshot (about 15 minutes)

  • Pause on one organism that catches your eye and take a screenshot. If the ROV's on-screen data shows depth and temperature, try to include it in the frame.
  • Check the Padlet first. If a classmate has already pinned the same animal at the same depth, pick a different one. The same type of animal at a different depth is welcome.
  • You don't need to know the animal's name. A clear description like "orange, five-armed sea star" is perfect.

Step 4: Pin your organism to the class water column (by Thursday of Week 4)

Post elementWhat to include
TitleA short description of the organism (for example, "Orange five-armed sea star").

Step 5: Comment on two classmates' pins (by Sunday of Week 4)

Comment on two classmates' posts, and make at least one of them in a different depth column from yours. Engage with their reasoning in 2–3 sentences. Starters: "Another condition that might explain this is… because…", "Compared with my animal at ___ m, yours…", "I wonder whether…"

Step 6: Write your Pattern Post (by Sunday of Week 4)

Once the board has filled in, scroll through the whole water column, top to bottom. Then add a post in the "Patterns We See" column (3–5 sentences):

  • Describe one pattern you notice across at least two depth zones: who lives there, how animals look or move, or how much life there is.
  • Explain which of the four conditions you think causes that pattern, and why.
  • Share one question you still have about life in the deep sea.

What happens next

I'll read the whole board, paying special attention to the posts marked "Guessing," the patterns you spot, and the questions you ask. On Monday of Week 5, I'll post a short video walking through our class water column, highlighting great observations, answering common questions, and clearing up mix-ups.

Summative assessment

Monster Files: Fact-Checking the Midnight Zone

Why this is here · draft annotation

This project replaces a traditional exam with a creative, fully asynchronous task built through backward design from LO2. Students fact-check six claims in a short media clip about a deep-sea "monster," then publish a field guide entry comparing the monster to the real animal. It targets Bloom's analyze and evaluate levels, builds academic integrity in through personal clip choice, quoted timestamps, and a clear AI policy, and leaves students with a skill they can use on any science story.

  • Unit 2
  • 50 points
  • LO2
  • Fully online, asynchronous
  • Due Sunday of Week 5

Course learning outcome

"Students will be able to distinguish scientific evidence from exaggeration/invention by comparing fictional creatures to the real organisms that inspired them." (LO2)

Statement of purpose

The purpose of this summative assessment is to evaluate your ability to distinguish scientific evidence from exaggeration and invention in popular portrayals of the deep sea. Movies, documentaries, and viral videos shape how most people picture the deep ocean, often more than science does. In this project, you'll act as both a fact-checker and a naturalist: you'll test a media portrayal of a deep-sea "monster" against scientific evidence, then show the public what the real animal behind the legend is like. This assessment allows you to demonstrate mastery of key course concepts and build a skill you can use on any science story long after this course ends.

Instructions

Complete a three-part project that fact-checks a short media clip about a deep-sea "monster" and compares the monster to the real organism that inspired it.

Choose your monster and clip

Pick one creature from the curated list below (or propose another by messaging me in Canvas Inbox by Wednesday of Week 4). Then find a short media clip (3–10 minutes) that portrays it, such as a documentary segment, a TikTok or YouTube Short, a film scene, or a "Megalodon still lives"-style piece. Choose a clip with captions when possible.

Fact-Check Log (23 points)

Identify 6 scientific claims in your clip and rate each one against the scientific literature as Supported, Exaggerated, or Invented. For each claim, record the timestamp, the exact line (quoted), your verdict, 2–4 sentences of evidence explaining what the science says, and your source(s). Include at least one claim in each verdict category. Claims must be scientific (biology, size, behavior, habitat, depth, history of discovery), not plot or opinion.

Field Guide Entry (15 points)

Create a one-page illustrated field guide entry that compares your monster side by side with the real animal that inspired it. If more than one real organism inspired your monster, choose the one that best matches your clip. Include:

  • An image of the monster as portrayed and an image of the real animal, both credited (Creative Commons or public-domain images, e.g., NOAA Ocean Exploration or MBARI)
  • Key facts about the real animal: size, depth range, habitat, and notable adaptations
  • A visual "Reality Meter" summarizing your 6 verdicts (use labels or symbols as well as color)
  • At least 3 labeled differences between the monster and the real animal
  • Alt text for each image (1–2 sentences)

Reflection (7 points)

In 250–400 words, explain which claim surprised you most and why; why you think the media exaggerated or invented the traits it did (consider entertainment, fear of the unknown, and audience expectations); and how this will change the way you watch science media in the future.

References and AI Use Note (5 points)

Include an APA reference list of all sources and images, and a 1–3 sentence AI Use Note.

Submission details

  • Use a minimum of 4 credible sources, including at least one peer-reviewed article, in APA style. Reputable sources include NOAA, MBARI, Smithsonian Ocean, Schmidt Ocean Institute, and museum or university research pages. Wikipedia, blogs, AI chatbots, and media clips cannot be used as evidence.
  • Field guide no longer than one page; reflection 250–400 words.
  • Submit two files in Canvas: (1) Fact-Check Log & Reflection (.docx or PDF, with clip link, references, and AI Use Note) and (2) Field Guide Entry (PDF).
  • Due Sunday of Week 5 at 11:59 PM Pacific Time. If you need more time, message me before the due date and we'll work out a plan.
  • Tools: Canvas, plus a free design tool of your choice (Canva for Education, Google Slides, Adobe Express, or PowerPoint through OSU's Microsoft 365).
  • Turnitin is enabled for the Fact-Check Log & Reflection. Quoted lines and properly cited material will show as matches, and that's expected.
  • AI tools may be used to brainstorm a monster or clip, generate search terms, help understand a difficult article, check grammar or layout, or create an illustration of the monster (labeled "AI-generated"). They may not be used to decide verdicts, write evidence explanations or the reflection, create images of the real animal, or serve as evidence.
  • Discuss monsters, clips, and sources with classmates in the Unit 2 Q&A, but claims, verdicts, field guide, and reflection must be your own. See OSU's Code of Student Conduct.
  • You'll receive rubric scores and written or audio feedback within 7 days of the due date.

Tips for success

  • Pick your clip early; short clips with lots of confident "facts" work best.
  • Finish your Fact-Check Log before designing your field guide, since the field guide is built from it.
  • "Exaggerated" means a real basis was stretched; "invented" means there's no scientific basis. If you're unsure, explain your reasoning.
  • Use the rubric as a checklist before you submit, and post questions in the Unit 2 Q&A discussion.
